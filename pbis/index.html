The Foundation

What Is PBIS in MEG's Mathematics Courses?

PBIS is not an add-on in Intermediate Algebra, Algebra 2, or Geometry. It isn't a separate curriculum, a poster you hang and ignore, or a set of extrinsic rewards disconnected from learning.

PBIS is built into the lesson DNA. Every component — the warm-up, the direct instruction, the guided practice, the Merit Time game, the exit ticket — is designed with behavioral expectations and natural reward moments embedded from the start.

This means teachers don't need to invent their own PBIS system or stop instruction to "do PBIS." The curriculum carries the framework — teachers deliver math and reinforce positive behavior simultaneously, every single lesson.

The Three Expectations

Ready. Respectful. Responsible.

Every MEG lesson — in Intermediate Algebra, Algebra 2, and Geometry — reinforces three universal expectations. These aren't abstract rules — they're concrete, observable behaviors tied to specific moments in the lesson structure.

Expectation

Be Ready

Students arrive mentally and materially prepared to engage with the math. Readiness is built into the lesson structure — not assumed.

Classroom Examples

  • Warm-Up Routine: Students enter to a displayed warm-up problem. Expectations set before the bell — materials out, bags away, focus on the board.
  • Transition Signals: "Pencils up in 5... 4... 3..." — clear verbal and visual cues for shifting activities.
  • Material Check: Guided notes and slide prompts remind students to have their supplies ready before independent work.
Expectation

Be Respectful

Students treat peers, teachers, and the learning environment with dignity. Respect is modeled in every interaction and reinforced through structured participation.

Classroom Examples

  • Think-Pair-Share Protocol: Structured partner talk with sentence starters — "I noticed..." "I wonder..." — ensuring every voice is heard.
  • Discussion Norms: Slide-deck-embedded norms for whole-group discussion — one voice at a time, build on ideas, disagree respectfully.
  • Merit Time Game Conduct: Clear expectations for turn-taking, celebrating peers' correct answers, and good sportsmanship.
Expectation

Be Responsible

Students own their learning, their materials, and their choices. Responsibility is taught explicitly and acknowledged consistently.

Classroom Examples

  • Guided Notes: Students complete fill-in-the-blank notes during instruction — building accountability for capturing and organizing their learning.
  • Self-Check Moments: Lessons embed "Pause and Reflect" slides where students self-assess their understanding — building metacognitive responsibility.
  • Exit Ticket Routine: Students complete a quick-check problem before leaving — taking ownership of demonstrating what they learned.

Aligned to Your School's Expectations

The three expectations — Be Ready, Be Respectful, Be Responsible — are designed to be compatible with virtually any school-wide PBIS system. If your school uses different language (Safe, Respectful, Responsible; or PAWS; or SOAR), the framework adapts. The Teacher Guide includes a matrix alignment tool that maps MEG's expectations to your school's existing language.

Natural Rewards

Rewards That Reinforce Learning

Effective PBIS rewards are built into the experience — not candy or prizes handed out independently of instruction. Every MEG mathematics course — Intermediate Algebra, Algebra 2, and Geometry — embeds natural reward moments that celebrate positive behavior while keeping students engaged in math.

Merit Time Games

Every lesson ends with Merit Time — a review game that makes math practice social, fun, and rewarding. Games are cooperative and competitive by design, giving students a natural payoff for engagement.

  • Team-Based: Students work in small groups, building collaboration and collective problem-solving.
  • Low-Stakes Practice: Wrong answers are learning moments, not penalties. Positive participation is the primary reward.
  • Celebration Built In: Game master slides include celebration prompts — class applause, team shout-outs, recognition points.

PBIS Bonuses in Game Masters

Game masters (the teacher-facing slide decks for Merit Time) include explicit PBIS bonus prompts that let teachers award recognition for positive behaviors during the game.

  • Bonus Point Prompts: "Bonus point for the team that showed the most respectful listening!" — pre-written, ready to use.
  • Sportsmanship Recognition: Slides cue teachers to highlight students who help teammates, share strategies, or encourage others.
  • Consistent Structure: Same bonus format every lesson — students know what's recognized and why.

Recognition Systems

Beyond individual lessons, every MEG course provides structures for ongoing recognition that connects classroom behavior to school-wide PBIS systems.

  • Ticket/Token Systems: Templates and guidance for a simple classroom economy tied to the three expectations.
  • Class Charts: Visual progress trackers for recognizing collective class achievement across lessons.
  • School-Wide Integration: Guidance for connecting classroom recognition to your school's existing PBIS reward system.

Classroom Management

Manage the Classroom, Teach the Math

Classroom management isn't separate from instruction — it's the container that makes instruction possible. MEG's Intermediate Algebra, Algebra 2, and Geometry provide structured routines and procedures so teachers spend less time managing behavior and more time teaching mathematics.

Daily Routines

Consistent Lesson Structure

Every lesson follows the same four-phase flow: Bell Ringer → Launch/Hook → Explore & Develop → Reflect & Practice (Merit Time + exit ticket). Students know what comes next — reducing anxiety and off-task behavior.

Embedded Time Cues

Slide decks include suggested timings for each segment. Teachers can use timers, verbal countdowns, or visual progress bars — the structure is built in.

Predictable Closures

Every lesson ends with an exit ticket, giving students a consistent signal that learning is wrapping up and providing a calm transition to the next class period.

Transition Procedures

Activity Transitions

Clear signals mark shifts between lesson segments: "Pencils down when you hear the chime. Eyes on me in 3... 2... 1..." Pre-written scripts in the lesson plans reduce cognitive load on teachers.

Group Work Formation

Pre-designed grouping strategies for Merit Time and partner work. Teachers don't need to invent grouping systems — the structure is provided, and PBIS expectations for group work are pre-taught.

Material Management

Guided notes and handouts follow a consistent format. Students learn where to find information, where to write responses, and how to organize their materials — reducing confusion and lost work.

Proactive Interventions

Pre-Correction Strategies

Lesson plans identify common behavior risk points and provide proactive prompts — "Before we begin partner work, let's review what respectful collaboration sounds like."

Active Supervision Cues

Teachers are prompted to circulate, provide specific praise, and use non-verbal redirection during independent and group work. The curriculum cues these moves at the right moments.

Re-Direct Scripts

Brief, respectful re-direction language is provided for common off-task behaviors — delivered calmly, focused on the expectation, never on the student's character.

For School Leaders

The MEG classroom management framework is designed to reduce the time teachers spend on behavior management by structuring predictability into every lesson. When teachers have clear routines and scripts, they spend less energy inventing management strategies and more energy building relationships and teaching math. New teachers, in particular, report that the structured routines help them establish positive classroom culture from day one.
